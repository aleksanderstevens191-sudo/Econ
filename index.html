Why it’s built this way

Each feature uses a study method with strong evidence behind it. The short version: testing yourself beats rereading, and spreading practice out beats cramming.

  • Guess first

    Each module opens with a question before you’ve read anything. Even a wrong guess makes the real answer stick better once you see it.

    Pretesting effect · Richland, Kornell & Kao, 2009
  • Small chunk, then a question

    Notes come in short pieces, each followed by a question. Pulling an answer out of memory strengthens it far more than reading it again.

    Retrieval practice · Roediger & Karpicke, 2006 · rated “high utility” by Dunlosky et al., 2013
  • Graphs that build as you listen

    The videos draw one piece of a graph at a time, with the words timed to the motion. Turn on Narrate to hear the words instead of reading them.

    Dual coding · Paivio · Mayer’s multimedia principles: segmenting, signaling, modality, temporal contiguity
  • Worked examples, then fewer hints

    You see a full solution, then solve a similar problem yourself, with a hint if your first try misses.

    Worked-example effect · Sweller & Cooper, 1985 · fading, Renkl & Atkinson, 2003
  • Explain it in your words

    Typing an explanation and comparing it with a model answer shows you exactly what you don’t understand yet.

    Self-explanation · Chi et al., 1994
  • Spaced, mixed review

    Missed questions come back right away. Correct ones return after 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days. Review sets mix modules, the way an exam does.

    Spacing · Cepeda et al., 2006 · Interleaving · Rohrer & Taylor, 2007
  • Unlimited practice exams

    Each generated exam uses new numbers, so you practice the method instead of remembering answers. Varied, effortful practice feels slower but carries over better to problems you haven’t seen.

    Practice testing · Dunlosky et al., 2013 · Desirable difficulties · Bjork, 1994
  • Brain dump at the end

    Before leaving a module, write everything you remember, then check it against the summary.

    Free recall · Karpicke & Blunt, 2011

Module 5

Markets: efficiency and equity

Who gets scarce goods, how much value a market creates, and whether the result is fair.

5.1 Not on the Midterm 2 list

Nine ways to hand out scarce things

Scarcity means something has to decide who gets what. Most goods use more than one method. Concert tickets have a price and a line.

MethodWorks best whenExample
Market pricePeople who value it most are willing and able to payPizza, shoes, haircuts

Key idea

Market price is the most efficient method: goods go to the people who value them most and can pay. Non-price methods are often seen as fairer.

5.2

Demand is marginal benefit

  • Value is what a good is worth to you, the most you’d pay. Price is what you actually pay.
  • The height of the demand curve at any quantity is the buyer’s willingness to pay for one more unit. That is marginal benefit, so demand = MB.
  • Market demand is the horizontal sum: add quantities at each price. At $6, Steve buys 30 boxes of cookies and Sara buys 15, so the market buys 45.
  • Consumer surplus (CS) is value minus price, added up over every unit. On a graph it’s the area below demand and above the price.

Worked example · Steve’s consumer surplus

Steve’s demand curve starts at $12 on the price axis. The price is $6 and he buys 30 boxes.

  1. Height of the triangle$12 − $6 = $6
  2. Base of the triangle30 boxes
  3. Consumer surplus½ × 30 × $6 = $90
  4. What he spends (the box under the price)$6 × 30 = $180
  5. Total value to Steve = CS + spending$90 + $180 = $270

Your turn. Same steps, new numbers.

5.3

Supply is marginal cost

  • Cost is what a seller gives up to produce. Price is what the seller receives.
  • The height of the supply curve is the minimum price the seller will accept for one more unit. That is marginal cost, so supply = MC.
  • Producer surplus (PS) is price minus marginal cost, added up. On a graph it’s the area above supply and below the price.
  • For sellers: total revenue (P × Q) minus total cost (the red triangle under supply) = PS. The course treats this as the seller’s profit.

Worked example · Barbara’s producer surplus

Barbara’s supply curve starts at $0. The price is $8 and she sells 30 boxes.

  1. Height of the triangle$8 − $0 = $8
  2. Producer surplus½ × 30 × $8 = $120
  3. Her 10th box: minimum price $2, sells for $8PS = $6
  4. Total revenue$8 × 30 = $240
  5. Total cost = TR − PS$240 − $120 = $120

Your turn.

5.4

When is a market efficient?

  • At equilibrium, MB = MC. Total surplus (CS + PS) is as big as it can be, and there’s no deadweight loss. Adam Smith called the force behind this the invisible hand.
  • Pareto efficient: you can’t make anyone better off without making someone else worse off.
  • Underproduction: less than equilibrium, so MB > MC. Course examples: rent control, immunizations, public highways.
  • Overproduction: more than equilibrium, so MC > MB. Course examples: pollution from electricity, overfishing.
  • Both create deadweight loss (DWL), surplus that nobody gets.
  • Six causes of market failure: price and quantity regulations, taxes and subsidies, high transaction costs, externalities, public goods and common resources, monopoly.

5.5

Is it fair? Results versus rules

An efficient market can still leave people out. At $6 a box, anyone who can’t afford $6 gets no cookies. Economists judge fairness two ways.

Fair resultsFair rules
Big ideaJudge the outcome: who ends up with what (distributive justice).Judge the process: same rules and equal opportunity for everyone.
ThinkersUtilitarians. John Rawls, A Theory of Justice (1971): make the poorest as well off as possible, without shrinking the pie, and no discrimination.Robert Nozick, Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974): protect property rights and voluntary exchange.
MeasureIncome inequality.Economic mobility.

Worked example · the utilitarian case

Because of diminishing marginal utility, an extra dollar matters more to a poorer person.

  1. Take $5,000 from someone earning $130,000−2 utils
  2. Give it to someone earning $20,000+10 utils
  3. Net change in total utility+8 utils
  4. Keep going until marginal utilities are equalMU₁ = MU₂

The catch: taxes are costly to run (complex tax code) and taxing work effort cuts output. Bigger transfers shrink the pie.

Module 6

Price ceilings, floors, taxes and subsidies

What happens when the government sets prices or adds a wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers keep.

Opener

Why do Super Bowl tickets resell for so much?

YearFace valueIn 2019 dollars
1967$12$92
1996about $350$559
2019$2,557resale average about $8,000

If fans happily pay about $8,000, the face value is below what the market would set. Scalpers may simply be finding the market-clearing price. Keep that question in mind for 6.1.

6.1

Price ceilings: rent control

  • A price ceiling is a legal maximum. A price floor is a legal minimum.
  • A ceiling only binds if it’s set below equilibrium. A floor only binds if it’s above. Non-binding ones do nothing.
  • A binding rent ceiling makes landlords cut back from Qe to Qs, so there’s a shortage.
  • With only Qs apartments, renters would pay a much higher P2 for the last one. That gap feeds black markets. In Seinfeld’s “The Andrea Doria,” George loses the apartment to someone who “just gave the super 50 bucks.”
  • Apartments get rationed by non-price methods: first-come, lottery, contests, even discrimination. Search activity raises the real cost above the legal rent.
  • Rent control: New York, San Francisco, London, Paris. None: Chicago, Atlanta, Dallas, Seattle.
Ceiling: low to bindA ceiling only hurts if it’s low enough to hit your head. Set below equilibrium: shortage.
Floor: high to bindA floor only trips you if it’s raised above where you stand. Set above equilibrium: surplus.

6.2

Price floors: the minimum wage

  • In the labor market the roles flip: workers supply labor and firms demand it.
  • The federal minimum wage (Fair Labor Standards Act) is a binding floor. A local living wage sits even higher.
  • At the floor, more people want jobs than firms will fill. That labor surplus is unemployment.
  • Employment is set by demand, so it’s underproduction: a yellow box of search costs and a gray DWL triangle. It hits the least-skilled workers hardest, though economists debate how much.
  • Farm price supports are a floor where the government buys the surplus.

6.3

Taxes: who really pays?

  • Tax incidence is how the burden of a tax splits between buyers and sellers.
  • Sin taxes (alcohol, tobacco, gambling, sugar) and gas taxes target goods with inelastic demand.
  • It doesn’t matter who the law taxes. Taxing buyers or sellers gives the same split.
  • The more inelastic side pays more. Income tax falls mostly on workers because labor supply is fairly inelastic.
  • A tax causes underproduction. Yellow box = tax revenue. Gray triangle = DWL. No DWL only if demand or supply is perfectly inelastic.
If this is trueWho pays the whole tax
Demand is perfectly inelasticBuyers
Demand is perfectly elasticSellers
Supply is perfectly inelasticSellers
Supply is perfectly elasticBuyers

Now with fewer hints · movie tickets

A $4 tax per ticket. Before: $10 and 200 tickets. After: buyers pay $11 and 160 tickets sell. Use the same steps as the pizza video.

6.4 Midterm 2: subsidies yes, quotas no

Subsidies and quotas

  • Subsidy ($6 per pizza to sellers): supply shifts right. Price falls from $8 to $5, quantity rises from 75 to 100, and sellers get $5 + $6 = $11. Now MSC $11 > MSB $5: overproduction and DWL. Example: farm subsidies.
  • Quota (T-shirt imports): equilibrium is $8 and 100,000 shirts. A 50,000 quota makes supply vertical at 50,000. Buyers pay $12, but the cost is $4. The $8 gap times 50,000 is quota rents (yellow box). MSB $12 > MSC $4: underproduction and DWL.

Before you leave Module 6

Brain dump

Write what you remember about ceilings, floors, tax incidence, subsidies and quotas. Then compare.

  • Binding ceiling: below equilibrium. Shortage, black markets, search costs, underproduction, DWL.
  • Binding floor: above equilibrium. Surplus. In labor markets the surplus is unemployment.
  • Tax: buyers’ price − sellers’ price = tax. Revenue = tax × new Q. DWL = ½ × ΔQ × tax.
  • Who the law taxes doesn’t matter. The more inelastic side pays more.
  • Perfectly inelastic demand or supply: no DWL.
  • Subsidy: overproduction (MSC > MSB). Quota: underproduction (MSB > MSC) with quota rents.

Module 7

Other market failures

Spillovers onto third parties, goods nobody can be kept from using, and resources that get used up.

7.1

Four kinds of market failure

A market fails when it produces too much or too little. The course groups the causes into four: fairness (Module 6), externalities, public goods and common resources. Market power comes later.

7.2

Negative externalities

  • An externality is a cost or benefit that spills onto a third party. Examples of costs: fracking, CO₂, smog, mercury.
  • MSC = MC + marginal external cost. The vertical gap between supply and MSC is the size of the externality.
  • The market ignores that cost, so it makes too much: overproduction, where MSC > MSB.
  • Study tip: the DWL triangle’s tip points toward the efficient quantity. Left tip = overproduction. Right tip = underproduction.
FixHow it worksCatch
Pigovian taxTax = marginal external cost at the efficient quantity (Arthur Pigou)Cheapest to run and monitor, so most economists prefer it
Emission chargesA price per unit of pollutionPollution is hard to measure
Marketable permitsCap total pollution, let firms trade permits, cut the cap over timeTransaction costs
Property rightsGive someone ownership so they bargain over itOnly works in some cases (see Coase)

7.3

The Coase theorem

Ronald Coase: if transaction costs are low and few parties are involved, it doesn’t matter who gets the property rights. The two sides will bargain their way to the efficient outcome.

Course example: a Louisiana gas driller and an environmental group can bargain. It breaks down with many parties, and nobody holds property rights to air, lakes or oceans.

7.4

Positive externalities

  • MSB = MB + marginal external benefit. Examples: education, vaccines (COVID-19).
  • Hershey, PA smells like chocolate: a plus for visitors, maybe a minus for residents.
  • The market ignores the benefit to others, so it makes too little: underproduction, where MSB > MSC.
  • Fixes: a subsidy or voucher equal to the external benefit (Pell Grants, state funding for IU), public provision, and patents and copyrights.

7.5

Four types of goods

Rival: one person’s use leaves less for others. Excludable: people who don’t pay can be kept out.

7.6

Public goods and free riders

  • Public goods are non-rival, non-excludable and indivisible. If neighbors ask you to chip in $20 for a park, you can enjoy it whether you pay or not. That’s the free-rider problem.
  • So markets underproduce public goods, and they get funded by taxes.
  • Market demand for a private good is the horizontal sum of individual demands. For a public good it’s the vertical sum. Efficient amount: MSB = MSC.

7.7 Midterm 2: the commons yes, Ostrom no

The tragedy of the commons

  • Northwest Atlantic cod were overfished in the 1970s and 1980s and collapsed by 1992. Each boat ignores the cost it puts on everyone else, so MSC > MSB and the resource is overused. It works like a negative externality, and the free-rider problem is worse than with public goods.
  • Fixes: (1) property rights such as fishing licenses, (2) a quota at the sustainable amount, handed out by a non-price method, (3) individual transferable quotas (IFQs) that work like marketable permits. Sometimes governments do nothing for political reasons.
  • Elinor Ostrom of IU won the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economics, the first woman to win it, for Governing the Commons (1990). Communities can manage shared resources themselves using eight design principles:
1Clearly defined boundaries5Graduated sanctions
2Rules that fit local conditions6Cheap, quick conflict resolution
3Collective-choice arrangements7Recognized right to organize
4Effective monitoring8Nested enterprises for larger systems

Firms, production and costs

What firms count as cost, how markets are structured, and why cost curves have the shapes they do.

8.1

Accounting profit vs economic profit

  • Firms aim to maximize profit. Accounting profit = sales minus bills paid. (Enron, 2001, shows how far books can drift from reality.)
  • Economic profit also subtracts opportunity costs.
  • Explicit costs come with a bill: dough, cheese, electricity, rent.
  • Implicit costs don’t, and they count only in economic profit.
  • Normal profit is the entrepreneur’s pay for running the firm. It’s part of cost.

Worked example · implicit costs of a pizza shop

  1. Depreciation: a $5,000 oven is worth $4,000 after a year$1,000
  2. Forgone interest: $5,000 of savings could earn 3%$150
  3. Owner’s time: a forgone neuroscientist salary$125,000
  4. Total implicit costs$126,150

8.2 Midterm 2: information yes, business forms no

Organizing production

  • Firms run on command systems (orders) or incentive systems (rewards). Information can be perfect or imperfect, symmetric or asymmetric.
  • Adverse selection = hidden information before a deal. Used cars (the seller knows more), health insurance (the buyer knows more).
  • Moral hazard = changed behavior after a deal. Driving aggressively once insured.
  • Principal-agent problem: owners and workers want different things. Fixes: ownership, profit sharing, bonuses, long-term contracts.

8.4

Short run vs long run

Short run: at least one input is fixed, usually plant size. Only labor can change. Long run: every input can change. It’s about flexibility, not a set amount of time.

Sunk costs are already spent and can’t be recovered, so they shouldn’t affect decisions.

8.5

Production and diminishing returns

  • Total product (TP): output from a given amount of labor and capital.
  • Average product AP = TP ÷ workers. Marginal product MP = change in TP from one more worker.
  • Diminishing returns: with a fixed kitchen, each new cook adds less. Too many cooks in the kitchen.
  • TP is an upside-down U that peaks where MP = 0. MP crosses AP at AP’s highest point. Keep hiring while MP > 0, never when MP < 0.

FormulaAt 2 units of corn (course example)

  • MC crosses AVC and ATC at their minimums. MC below an average pulls it down. MC above pulls it up.
  • Higher productivity or a lower wage shifts AVC, ATC and MC down. AFC doesn’t move.

8.7

Marginal cost mirrors marginal product

MC = wage ÷ MP. With a $10 wage, a worker who adds 10 pizzas makes MC $1 a pizza. One who adds 5 pizzas makes MC $2.

So when MP rises, MC falls, and when MP falls, MC rises. They’re mirror images. Rising MP also means falling AVC.

8.8

The long run: choosing plant size

  • The long-run average cost (LRAC) curve is the lower envelope of the short-run ATC curves for each plant size. For each output, pick the plant with the lowest cost.
  • Economies of scale (specialization, cheaper inputs and borrowing, technology): LRAC falls.
  • Constant returns to scale: the flat bottom.
  • Diseconomies of scale (bureaucracy): LRAC rises.
  • Diminishing returns does not explain LRAC. It needs a fixed input, and the long run has none.

8.9 · on the Midterm 2 list

Revenue: total, marginal and average

  • Total revenue: TR = P × Q.
  • Marginal revenue is the extra revenue from selling one more unit: MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ. It’s the revenue twin of marginal cost.
  • Average revenue is revenue per unit: AR = TR ÷ Q, which is just the price.
  • When a firm has to cut its price to sell more, like the monopoly in practice question 21, MR drops below the price and can turn negative. TR peaks where MR reaches zero.
